# Composition Geometry for 2.39:1 Frames

Choose geometry to serve the director and narrative. Never place a visible grid, guide, spiral, or diagram in the generated image.

## Normalized Frame Coordinates

Treat the final 2.39:1 raster as normalized coordinates: left/top = 0, right/bottom = 1.

- Crop-safe region: keep critical faces, eyes, hands, and decisive objects within x = 0.05–0.95 and y = 0.08–0.92.
- Rule-of-thirds nodes: x = 0.333 or 0.667; y = 0.333 or 0.667.
- Golden-section nodes: x = 0.382 or 0.618; y = 0.382 or 0.618.
- Center axis: x = 0.5; useful for symmetry, one-point perspective, ritual, confrontation, or deliberate stasis.

Use coordinates as planning aids only. Convert them into natural language placement in the final prompt.

## Geometry Library

### Rule of Thirds

- Place the primary face/action at one thirds intersection.
- Place a secondary character, object, practical light, or exit on the opposing intersection or line.
- Reserve the remaining third as active look room, travel space, or emotional absence.
- Best for observational realism, balanced asymmetry, landscape scale, and readable two-shots.

### Golden Section

- Place the primary anchor near a 0.382/0.618 intersection.
- Use the opposite golden line for a doorway, horizon, body edge, or secondary action.
- Best for restrained asymmetry that should feel organic rather than diagrammatic.

### Golden Spiral

- Place the emotional focal point near a golden-section node.
- Arrange foreground shape, body curve, architecture, light, or gaze so the eye turns through two or three secondary details toward that point.
- State the visible path, not “add a golden spiral.”
- Best for intimate discovery, longing, suspense, or a decisive object revealed through layered depth.

### Bilateral Symmetry and One-Point Perspective

- Put the vanishing point and dominant subject on or deliberately near the center axis.
- Balance architectural mass and light on both sides; introduce one controlled asymmetry when the narrative requires tension.
- Best for ritual, systems, deadpan theatricality, dread, or exact confrontation.

### Diagonal and Triangular Balance

- Build a strong diagonal from foreground to subject to background, or a triangle among three story-bearing anchors.
- Keep the horizon intentional and prevent diagonal props from cutting through eyes or necks.
- Best for urgency, unstable relationships, urban energy, or precise tension.

### Frame Within a Frame

- Use doors, windows, mirrors, shelves, curtains, or architectural openings to contain or separate the subject.
- Align the inner frame with a thirds or golden line unless strict centrality is director-specific.
- Keep borders from grazing heads, hands, or faces.
- Best for domestic observation, emotional separation, memory, surveillance, and layered social space.

### Layered Depth Tableau

- Assign a foreground guide, midground action, and background consequence.
- Give each plane a distinct tonal value, focus role, or movement behavior.
- Connect the planes using gaze, gesture, perspective lines, light, or repeated shape.
- Best for ensemble life, social systems, family space, history, and patient observation.

### Dominant Negative Space

- Keep the subject small or off-center and make the empty region point toward, isolate, or pressure the subject.
- Give empty space texture, light gradient, architecture, weather, or narrative direction; never leave it inert.
- Best for isolation, scale, absence, anticipation, and emotional distance.

### Axial Offset Symmetry

- Build an almost-symmetrical frame around a center axis, then displace the subject, clue, or light by 3–10% of frame width.
- Let the broken balance create psychological or narrative pressure; do not use accidental asymmetry.
- Best for controlled tension, institutional order, moral imbalance, or a system beginning to fail.

### Nested Rectangles

- Place the subject through two or three receding frames such as doors, windows, mirrors, train compartments, shelves, or columns.
- Vary the tonal value and scale of each rectangle so the eye travels inward without visual tangencies.
- Best for surveillance, entrapment, memory layers, social systems, and subjective discovery.

### Lateral Compartment Staging

- Divide the 2.39:1 width into two to five readable bays using walls, windows, columns, curtains, or pools of light.
- Assign the primary action to one bay and a consequence, absence, or repeated routine to another.
- Best for domestic ritual, deadpan order, simultaneous urban life, and characters sharing space without connection.

### S-Curve Eye Path

- Use a road, rail, body line, smoke, curtain, water edge, crowd flow, or repeated light to bend the eye through depth.
- Put the primary anchor at the curve's final turn and limit intermediate stops to two.
- Best for lyrical movement, landscape memory, processions, suspense, and gradual visual revelation.

### Extreme Foreground Scale

- Place one object, hand, shoulder, architectural edge, or landscape form very close to the camera while keeping the primary action readable deeper in frame.
- Make the size contrast causal: concealment, threat, intimacy, witness, class, or physical urgency.
- Best for wide-lens proximity, subjective pressure, physical action, and social observation.

### Strong Horizon and Vanishing Wedge

- Lock a deliberate horizon height, then use roof, road, rail, wall, shoreline, or shadow edges to create a wedge toward the primary or secondary anchor.
- Choose high horizon for pressure, low horizon for sky/scale, and centered horizon for confrontation or stasis.
- Best for landscape scale, waiting, pursuit, ritual approach, and figures facing large systems.

### Compressed Graphic Planes

- Use a long lens to stack bodies, weather, architecture, banners, vehicles, foliage, or lights into distinct overlapping planes.
- Separate planes with value, color, atmosphere, or motion direction rather than shallow bokeh alone.
- Best for surveillance, crowds, weather, ceremonial repetition, confrontations, and social density.

## Focal Hierarchy

Define these before prompting:

1. **Primary anchor:** the first place the viewer must look, usually eyes, hands, or the decisive object.
2. **Secondary anchor:** the clue that changes the meaning of the primary anchor.
3. **Eye path:** the foreground, gaze, line, light, repeated shape, or perspective path connecting them.
4. **Negative-space role:** look room, absence, threat, scale, anticipation, or emotional distance.

Limit the frame to one primary and one secondary anchor. Background details may support the path but must not compete.

## Crop-Safe Composition

- Generate with all critical anchors inside the normalized crop-safe region.
- Keep extra breathing room around the intended 2.39:1 content when the generator may return a nearby aspect ratio.
- After exact cropping, re-check eye room, headroom, hands, frame-within-frame borders, vanishing point, horizon, and the primary/secondary anchor relationship.
- Shift the crop anchor rather than stretching the raster.

## Composition Failure Gate

Regenerate once when any condition is true:

- no clear first focal point within one second;
- two faces or objects compete without narrative purpose;
- important eyes, hands, or objects sit on accidental crop edges;
- a doorway, shelf, horizon, or prop creates a tangent through a face or neck;
- negative space does not create direction, pressure, scale, or absence;
- the composition formula contradicts the director's visual grammar;
- the image would lose its appeal if color grading and texture were removed;
- exact 2.39:1 cropping breaks the intended balance or eye path.
